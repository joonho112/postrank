Monte Carlo uncertainty and diagnostics

Source: vignettes/m6-uncertainty-diagnostics.Rmd
m6-uncertainty-diagnostics.Rmd

Posterior uncertainty, numerical approximation error and model adequacy refer to different aspects of an analysis. An accurately computed posterior may leave the ordering of two entities uncertain. Similarly, optimization can be accurate for coefficients that are themselves estimated imprecisely. We distinguish these sources of uncertainty when interpreting the available diagnostics.

The calculations use two small bundled inputs: IID draws from a specified normal law and MCMC output with recorded chain history from a known two-state law. Both are simulation examples rather than empirical studies, and neither model fitting nor optimization is performed here.

Pairwise Monte Carlo standard errors

For the claim that entity i has higher priority than j on criterion k, the directional-error probability is the posterior mean of theta[i,k] <= theta[j,k]. A probability near one half describes uncertainty about that direction under the supplied law. A Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) describes uncertainty in estimating the probability from a finite simulation. Increasing the simulation size can reduce MCSE without making the posterior direction any more certain.

The object postrank_toy contains 32 IID whole-state draws for three fictional sites and two criteria. The following calculation selects the need_A comparison from 001 to site-B; the diagnostic therefore applies to this specified pair rather than to every pair.

library(postrank)
pair <- data.frame(entity_i = "001", entity_j = "site-B",
  criterion_id = "need_A")
iid_diagnostic <- postrank_diagnose(postrank_toy, pairs = pair)
pair_summary <- iid_diagnostic$summaries$pair_1
z_iid <- iid_diagnostic$traces$pair_1$values
iid_table <- data.frame(
  Draws = length(z_iid), Estimated_probability = mean(z_iid),
  MCSE = pair_summary$diagnostics$mcse_mean$value,
  Direct_SD_over_sqrt_S = sd(z_iid) / sqrt(length(z_iid)))
knitr::kable(iid_table, digits = 4)
Draws Estimated_probability MCSE Direct_SD_over_sqrt_S
32 0.25 0.0778 0.0778

For IID output, the MCSE is the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the number of retained states. For a binary trace this is sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / (S - 1)). It estimates pointwise numerical precision and supplies neither a simultaneous interval over pairs nor a probability that the grade report is correct. The small draw count is used for illustration and is not a recommended simulation size.

A posterior interval describes the target distribution under the model, whereas an interval for Monte Carlo error concerns the numerical estimate of a functional of that distribution. The distinction is developed in Chapter 15, “Monte Carlo error under a declared sampling law”.

Loss traces for a fixed report

For a fixed grade vector, postrank_diagnose() also forms criterion error and risk traces. With joint draws, it combines criterion losses within each same posterior state to obtain a weighted total-loss trace. This preserves their covariance, which generally precludes combining separate criterion MCSEs under an independence assumption.

The next example uses pr_toy_mcmc, four independent chains of 512 iterations each, initialized from their stationary law. Its producer uses transition probabilities .012 from state 0 to state 1 and .048 in reverse. Thus the stationary state-1 probability is .2, but successive states are strongly correlated. The data are stored chain by chain, with no warmup or thinning. These sampling properties are specified by the generator and cannot be inferred from an array without its chain records.

law <- attr(pr_toy_mcmc, "law")
mcmc <- postrank_draws(pr_toy_mcmc, regime = "mcmc", chains = 4L,
  warmup_per_chain = 0L, thin = 1L, declarations = law$declarations)
grade <- c(A = 1L, B = 2L, C = 3L)
settings <- list(weights = c(k1 = .6, k2 = .4), tau = .25)
diagnostic <- postrank_diagnose(mcmc, grade = grade, settings = settings,
  pairs = data.frame(entity_i = "A", entity_j = "B", criterion_id = "k1"))
selected_summaries <- diagnostic$summaries[c("pair_1", "weighted_total_loss")]
diagnostic_table <- do.call(rbind, lapply(selected_summaries, function(s)
  data.frame(Mean = s$mean, MCSE = s$diagnostics$mcse_mean$value,
    ESS_mean = s$diagnostics$ess_mean$value,
    Rhat = s$diagnostics$rhat$value,
    ESS_tail = s$diagnostics$ess_tail$value)))
rownames(diagnostic_table) <- c("A above B: k1 error", "Fixed report: weighted loss")
knitr::kable(diagnostic_table, digits = 4)
Mean MCSE ESS_mean Rhat ESS_tail
A above B: k1 error 0.1724 0.0576 42.9548 1.0905 NA
Fixed report: weighted loss 0.2482 0.0384 42.9548 1.0905 NA

The pair trace is exactly the state-1 indicator: its pooled mean is 353/2048 = 0.1724, versus .2 under the known law. The fixed report’s loss is 2/15 + (2/3) * indicator, so its exact expected loss is 4/15. Its estimated loss is 0.2482. The MCSE scales by 2/3 as well. This simple identity checks the diagnostic against the underlying states; it does not claim that this report is optimal.

Criterion traces remain available separately as diagnostic$summaries$criterion_risk_1 and criterion_risk_2. With criterionwise inputs, criterion diagnostics can be available while a joint total-loss MCSE is unavailable: separate blocks do not establish their simulation covariance. For minimax, the returned weighted total trace is auxiliary; it is not an implemented MCSE for the maximum of expected criterion risks.

Interpretation of MCMC diagnostics

For eligible equal-length chains the package calls posterior::rhat, ess_bulk, ess_tail, ess_mean, and mcse_mean. The installed posterior methods use split chains: R-hat combines rank-normalized and folded checks, bulk ESS concerns the rank-normalized distribution, and tail ESS uses the smaller of the .05 and .95 quantile ESS values. Mean ESS describes precision of the mean; mcse_mean uses the trace’s sample SD divided by its square root. These scalar diagnostics are not the multivariate determinant ESS described in Chapter 15, “Multivariate output analysis and effective sample size”.

For this binary trace, tail diagnostics encounter degenerate quantile indicators. The table retains the resulting NA. Metric records include available, unavailable_reason, result_kind, warnings and the method; unavailable is not zero. Similarly, an observed constant trace can mean that a rare state has never been visited. Zero empirical variation is not proof of zero posterior probability or adequate exploration.

The supplied history preserves original chain IDs and retained iteration numbers. Unequal chains keep every value in their pooled means, but the package does not calculate cross-chain MCSE, ESS or R-hat by truncating or padding them. One chain cannot establish independent-chain agreement. The package’s R-hat 1.01 and ESS 100-per-chain guidelines flag potential limitations in exploration; they do not establish convergence or finite-sample coverage.

In this run, R-hat is 1.0905, above 1.01. Mean and bulk ESS are both 42.95, below the guideline of 400 across four chains. The three stored warnings concern chain exploration and mean precision and are available in diagnostic$warnings.

chain_means <- diagnostic$summaries$pair_1$chain_means

The IID reference is substantially smaller because it ignores serial dependence. Substituting an estimated ESS for S in an IID concentration inequality is not justified. The dependent-chain theorem in the book instead requires stationarity, reversibility and a justified full-chain spectral-gap bound. Neither an empirical ESS nor a chosen burn-in supplies these constants, and the implementation does not provide a general finite-sample certificate for MCMC output.

Fixed and selected actions

The grade above was supplied explicitly. When a fit is supplied instead, the diagnostic verifies its input and grade identities, uses its settings, and marks the summaries as descriptive after selection from the same draws. Pointwise MCSE for that realized grade does not automatically give coverage after searching for the best grade. Even an explicit grade declaration does not let the software verify how the analyst selected it.

If every comparison is withheld, ordinary weighted loss is the constant tau at every state. There is then no loss variation to diagnose, although the latent targets may remain uncertain and chains may mix poorly. The object records structural_constant_from_action; conditional error ratios remain NA because N=0. Constant-action loss is different from an observed constant pair indicator.

Probability-only inputs and supplied marginal means or intervals contain no recoverable simulation history. Chain records and MCSE therefore cannot be recovered from these inputs. Typed mixture diagnostics, when supported with genuine hyperstate history, describe integration of conditional expected risk, not latent target-loss dispersion. Fixed Gaussian or finite-mixture representations do not by themselves imply a Monte Carlo sample count. See Posterior inputs for those distinctions.

Sensitivity analysis and cross-fitting

postrank_mc_fragility() compares the estimated weighted loss from disclosure in a pair’s better direction with the withholding cost. The absolute difference from tau is compared with a multiple of the MCSE of that direction’s weighted indicator. The grade report is chosen jointly, so a fragile pair need not change a grade. Screening and multiplier choices are reported. The default MCMC screen margin .1 is a computational screening rule and does not bound the omitted pairs.

Stability requests in postrank_diagnose() run additional optimizations on declared prefixes, chain subsets or settings. Overlapping subsets are not independent replications. Changed grades can reflect numerical noise, policy changes, multiple optima or optimization gaps; unchanged grades do not prove convergence. The record keeps failed and no-incumbent attempts alongside successful ones. These solves are optional and are not run in this article.

postrank_crossfit() instead selects a grade on each of two folds and evaluates it on the other. IID inputs use stored halves; MCMC uses groups of whole chains, or halves when only one chain exists. With independent target-IID folds and loss settings fixed independently of evaluation draws, out-of-fold linear weighted risk is conditionally unbiased for the training fold’s selected grade. Its difference from in-fold risk concerns that fold grade, not the optimism of the full-data grade.

The argument does not extend to the nonlinear maximum used by minimax. For MCMC, splitting whole chains alone establishes neither independence nor stationarity. Cross-fitting reuses the supplied fitted model; it is not refitting on held-out observations or testing external calibration. If both folds choose the same grade, signed differences cancel, so zero mean optimism need not mean precision; mean_abs_optimism retains scale. The fold statuses identify how many evaluations contributed to the summary; a result from one available fold supplies only one evaluation. Neither summary corrects the full-data risk.

Conditional IID coefficient bounds

For fixed-size IID sampling, a simultaneous coefficient envelope can cover an action selected using those same coefficients. The package’s tie-free setting makes reverse directions complementary. With M = choose(n, 2) and positive prespecified allocations summing to at most alpha, its criterion radius is

εk=min⁡{1,log⁡(2M/αk)2Sk}. \varepsilon_k = \min\left\{1, \sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\alpha_k)}{2S_k}}\right\}.

The broader equality-permitting theorem in the book counts both ordered directions and uses 4M, not 2M. Its scope must not be inferred from the runtime’s smaller factor. The separate equality-neutral mode does not currently supply these diagnostic or bound helpers.

The bundled normal generator prespecified S=32 and the following error allocations before sampling. These design declarations are therefore available for this example:

bounds <- postrank_iid_bounds(postrank_toy, alpha = .05,
  alpha_by_criterion = c(need_A = .025, need_B = .025),
  design = list(sample_sizes_fixed_before_sampling = TRUE,
    alpha_fixed_before_sampling = TRUE, adaptive_stopping = FALSE,
    planned_sample_sizes = c(need_A = 32L, need_B = 32L),
    declaration_note = "Prespecified in the bundled synthetic Normal generator"))

The probability statement is over repeated fixed-size Monte Carlo runs conditional on the supplied law and observed information. It is not a posterior probability that a grade is right. Draws must be independent across whole states within each criterion; pairs and criteria need not be independent of one another. Sampling sizes and error allocations must be fixed in advance, with no adaptive stopping. A declaration records these assumptions; it does not verify them. The result is a floating reference conditional_bound; formal guarantee and numerical-verification flags remain false.

On such a coefficient event, write delta_w = sum(w * epsilon). Risk error for a fixed action is at most (N/M) * delta_w. For the same fixed action family, an empirical optimizer with a valid whole-problem absolute risk gap eta has supplied-law regret at most 2 * delta_w + eta. postrank_regret_bound() checks eligible weighted fits, matching inputs and usable gap information; it does not turn a candidate-only, raw or relative gap into that eta. The bound concerns risk, not agreement of grade vectors. It excludes empirically protected problems from this common-family calculation.

Protection needs a separate construction: inner rows use E_hat_k <= (q_k - epsilon_k) * N, outer rows use the plus sign. The signed thresholds are not clipped to zero. A negative inner threshold can exclude all positive disclosure without proving the intended protected problem infeasible. Zero-weight protected criteria still need their own radii. Protection analysis combines a validated inner action with a lower bound for the whole outer problem, not the inner lower bound or outer incumbent. These additional optimizations are not run in this article. The helpers retain diagnostic rather than formal certification flags. Full derivations and the current implementation distinction appear in Chapter 15 and Appendix D.

Numerical accuracy and model adequacy

Solver gaps provide numerical bounds on suboptimality for the declared problem. MCSEs and coefficient envelopes quantify aspects of its approximation to the supplied law. These measures do not assess the difference between that law and an external population: greater numerical precision can coexist with model misspecification. The distinction is examined in Chapter 18, “Self-reported error and calibration”.

In plug-in empirical Bayes, posterior calculations condition on the fitted mixing distribution, often written G_hat. This is not the grade cap G. More posterior draws with G_hat fixed do not include uncertainty from estimating it. Hierarchical integration propagates the uncertainty specified by that model, but does not establish its external calibration. A repeated-data evaluation of a fitting procedure must repeat estimation, transformations and data-dependent settings, rather than freeze them across datasets.

Simulation results concern their stated generating laws and fitting rules. Independent datasets, not pairs or posterior arms from the same dataset, supply independent replications. Such results do not furnish a universal bias correction or make small reported risks calibrated in a new application. Diagnostics inform numerical work; assessment of targets, measurement and model adequacy requires the scientific evidence described in Chapter 18, “Hyperparameters, precision and represented uncertainty”.

Computing grades describes numerical statuses and gaps. True ties and withheld comparisons distinguishes shared grades from latent equality.
